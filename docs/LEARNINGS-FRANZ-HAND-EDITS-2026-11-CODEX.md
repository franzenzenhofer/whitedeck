## 2. Why his version is better

- **Management gets the decision before the methodology.** The cover and offer establish who did the work and what was commissioned. The next three slides give executives the trend, the consultant’s diagnosis, and the size of the opportunity. The detailed evidence remains available for the people who need to challenge or implement it.
- **The audience can see what the speaker is pointing at.** Bigger pie legends, Search Console totals, event markers and example screenshots let the client inspect the basis for a conclusion as Franz explains it. The higher-placed screenshots also shorten the jump between title and evidence. The same principle applies to larger text on methodology slides.
- **A strong takeaway makes a dense slide usable.** The audience can recall “4.7 days, rated good” and “825 ms, above the 400 ms maximum” without reconstructing the calculations. The added bullets help most when they compress a pattern across several rows or a long period, as on p40 and p55.
- **The chapter and audit sequences make recommendations easier to implement.** The MUI sequence shows the behavior, explains the implementation, and ends with checks a developer can run. The article sequence shows what the client’s pages actually contain, then supplies the checklist and publishing target. The two video demos make the proposed work concrete enough to discuss scope.
- **The presentation is more decisive, but some claims became more certain than the evidence warrants.** The crawl breakdowns flag reasonable investigation targets. Redirect share alone leaves the source of redirects open, and JavaScript request share alone leaves cache behavior open. The reason Google exposes impressions in the shown AI report and the proposed advertising-spend explanation also remain hypotheses. The final pages still have a few production issues worth catching, including the clipped source lines, crowded audit footer on **p187**, and broken sentence fragments on **p172**.

## 3. General rules for the generator

### Every deck

Type targets below use a **1920 × 1080 pt reference canvas**. Scale by canvas height. “Body” means the space between the header and the reserved source footer.

1. **Open a strategy deck with a decision brief.** After the title and any required scope evidence, put the key finding, the leading explanations and the outlook before the detailed analysis. Include one legible proof visual with each major summary claim when the evidence supports it. **Evidence:** HIS p1–6.
2. **Size primary charts for the room.** For a pie beside commentary, set its diameter to **at least 55% of body height**, targeting **60–65%**, and its legend to **at least 28 pt**. Keep the entire legend visible. For a two-period column chart above a table, start at **40% of slide width** and **36% of slide height**, including labels. **Evidence:** HIS p8, p30, p36, p38, p51, p58.
3. **Give screenshots space according to what the audience must read.** Start a landscape dashboard at **55–65% of slide width**, a tall single screenshot at **70% of body height or more**, and a row of two or three phone captures at **about 20–22% of slide width per capture**. Enlarge or crop to the evidence cited in the takeaway. Keep comparisons at a consistent scale. **Evidence:** HIS p18–19, p34–35, p139, p217, p231, p244.
4. **Set body type by layout and density.** Use **32 pt minimum** for main bullets, **36–44 pt** for short text-only findings and contents, and **1.15–1.30×** line spacing. Keep body text inside the reserved body rectangle. Use a wider text area or a continuation slide when it overflows. **Evidence:** HIS p6, p59, p93–94, p98, p155, p172, p296.
5. **Preserve the evidence in imported slides.** Reuse the approved slide or source artwork, with its charts, screenshots, diagrams, captions and links. Flag an import if a visual present in the source disappears in the output, or if headings and captions collapse into body bullets. **Evidence:** OURS p206–211 → HIS p217, p220, p228–229, p231, p244.
6. **Put a finding beside its proof.** Give each evidence slide one primary interpretation. Include period, denominator or benchmark when it changes the reading. For measured shortfalls, highlight the client value and benchmark together. Preserve separate wording for direct observations, diagnoses and forecasts. **Evidence:** HIS p40, p55, p58, p67, p98, p101, p155, p158, p160–162, p167–168.
7. **Deduplicate before export.** For adjacent slides with the same metric, period and chart, keep one visual and carry over every distinct supported finding. Keep a later reference to the same chart when it serves a separate audience function, as in an executive summary. Put a generic benchmark beside the client calculation when they fit legibly. **Evidence:** OURS p37–38 → HIS p40, OURS p153–154 → HIS p155, HIS p3 and p40.
8. **Build case studies through to acceptance.** For a major implementation recommendation, include observed behavior, relevant guidance, a worked example, failure modes and a pass/fail test. State sample size and test conditions beside the result. For an audit that leads to action, link the overview, the underlying examples and the proposed work. **Evidence:** HIS p184–188, p215–246, p304–305.
9. **Validate the rendered presentation.** Reserve the bottom **7% of slide height** for sources and the footer. Fail export review on clipped or covered text, broken links, unexplained blank slides, and contents entries with missing destinations. Permit intentional section breaks explicitly. **Evidence:** HIS p6, p18, p35, p172, p180, p187.

## 4. What to change on the first build, ranked by impact

1. **Generate the executive brief and action slides from the same findings as the analysis.** Give each finding an evidence reference, a measured value, an interpretation and a proposed action where applicable. Use those records to draft p3–5 and p304–305 automatically. This addresses the largest gap in the client story.
2. **Import MUI as a versioned slide module.** Preserve its original slide objects or artwork and bind the engagement’s examples and test details to them. This addresses all six of the largest visual changes and most of the insertions in one step.
3. **Replace the fixed body layout with content-specific templates.** Use distinct templates for a chart with a takeaway, a dashboard screenshot, a text-led finding, a table, and a side-by-side comparison. Render them with the size and footer checks above. This tackles the repeated manual enlargements across the deck.
4. **Plan the slide sequence before laying out the slides.** Consolidate duplicate charts, move the audit evidence ahead of the recommendations, pair benchmarks with client results, and regenerate the contents from the final order.
5. **Run an editorial review against the evidence, then a visual review of the export.** Complete clipped or awkward copy, check that each numeric claim uses the right scope, and flag any causal wording that goes beyond the evidence. Review at presentation size, including slide sources and the most detailed labels.

## 5. What to ask Franz or leave to him

- **The client-facing diagnosis.** Ask which findings he wants to defend as contributors to traffic loss and how strongly. The generator can draft from the measurements. Assigning weight to migration, rendering, demand and AI search requires his judgment.
- **The forecast and commercial commitment.** Have him approve the **20–30%** estimate, its baseline and KPI, the 12-month horizon, the Discover planning range, and how much to invest in video. The deck shows his estimates, but the screenshots supply no forecasting model that would justify reusing those numbers on another client.
- **The recommendation and the level of detail.** Ask whether this audience needs the full MUI walkthrough and which implementation and consent wording Franz is prepared to present. Similarly, let him choose which content formats fit each page and how ambitious a publishing target the client can sustain.
- **The strength and tone of the verdict.** The generator can propose concise interpretations and mark the evidence behind them. Franz should own judgments such as whether a rendering risk deserves a red callout, whether to raise advertising spend as an explanation, and which findings merit time in the live presentation.
